## Brad's Product Paradigms (his ten beliefs about product leadership)
The core four he leads with are 1, 3, 6 and 9.
1. Direction beats speed. AI collapsed the cost of building, so engineering now outruns discovery; the scarce work is deciding what is worth building and making sure it sells. Proof: built an operating model with two explicit gates ("Invest?" and "Build?") and still cut time to market from about 70 days to under 45. Soundbite: "Speed is table stakes now. The question is whether we're fast at the right things."
2. Outcomes, not features. Teams own outcomes, not feature lists; the roadmap names the outcome being moved, and features earn a place after the problem is understood. Proof: organized the portfolio around outcome pillars; those teams delivered $100M+ over five years.
3. Strategy is what we won't do. A strategy that can't say no is a wish list; good strategy is a few bets, each with a named sacrifice and a real diagnosis. Proof: held further investment in a major platform until it was clear where users needed value; every other bet scored on Impact, Effort and Confidence. Soundbite: "Show me what you decided not to do, and I'll tell you whether you have a strategy."
4. Complaints are evidence, not requirements. A request tells you where to look, not what to build. Proof: turned scattered seller complaints into one measurable problem, the first 90 days, and lifted early productivity 5%; at Kapow, recurring questions became a knowledge base that cut support 40%.
5. Prototypes are questions. A short brief, an opportunity statement and a prototype move faster than a long spec; every prototype states the question it answers. Proof: replaced PRDs with initiative briefs, prototypes and technical guardrails across a product organization.
6. Demos aren't products. A polished prototype looks finished, but behind it sit edge cases, data, permissions, performance and, with AI, evals and cost. Proof: clearing "Build?" still means going through Definition; when speed matters more than polish, work ships as a pilot with an explicit learning frame. Soundbite: "A demo proves the idea can work. Production proves customers can trust it."
7. Invisible AI. The best AI disappears into the experience; customers should notice the product knows what they need, not "the AI feature." Proof: put AI under the surface of a long-term product vision: personalized inspiration for shoppers and time back for sellers.
8. Transformation is a people problem. Licenses don't change how people decide or trust output; AI programs stall on ownership and culture, so he fixes those first and makes failed experiments safe. Proof: led a 40+ person, multi-year, $10M digital transformation; helped stand up an AI committee.
9. Builder-executive. Senior leaders still have to build: run the function, hire and coach, and stay close enough to prototype. Proof: ran discovery and built mock-ups himself as Sr. Director; built his Command Center, his website on his own design system, and this site's AI assistant. Soundbite: "I lead through others, but I never stopped building. Ask me what I made last month."
10. Rent before you buy. Prove value cheaply (A/B tests, prototypes with real users, small pilots) before committing money, headcount or roadmap. Proof: the "Invest?" gate exists for this; recommendation algorithms ran as continuous A/B tests.

## How he runs product
- I-D3-M operating model: Intake, Discovery, Definition, Development, Measure, with two gates: "Should we invest?" after Discovery and "Should we build?" after Definition. Product owns the outcome measured at the end.
- Initiative briefs, opportunity statements, prototypes and technical guardrails replace PRDs and detailed requirements.
- Every bet is scored on Impact, Effort and Confidence and tied to an outcome pillar.
- Quarterly strategy review, run like a board meeting for product: half a day to a full day with product leadership and research, data, design and engineering leads (under 20 people). Covers strategy and metrics, the cumulative list of results (failed tests count when they teach), each product area's one key metric and one-page strategy, then open issues, owners and next steps. Not a decision gate: winning tests ship before it. Targets roughly 75% optimization and 25% big bets, and keeps a killed list so dropped work stays dropped.
- Believes AI is collapsing the lines between product, UX, engineering and architecture, so discovery has to speed up.
- Reads Marty Cagan's SVPG, Jenny Wanger and TLDR Product; books that shaped him include Good Strategy Bad Strategy, Thinking in Systems, Power to the Middle and The Five Dysfunctions of a Team.
